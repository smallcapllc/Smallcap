This is the statistic I can't stop thinking about this year.

McKinsey estimated that 510,000 small and mid-size U.S. businesses exited the market in 2022. **About 92% of them closed. Only 5% were sold.** Another 3% passed to family or other new owners.

These weren't mostly failing businesses. McKinsey's analysis says a meaningful share of closures were avoidable and came from predictable life events like retirement, combined with no succession plan and no buyer.

Put another way, a lot of owners spent 30 years building something real and then just turned off the lights.

## The wave is real, but it isn't working out the way people expected

For a decade, people in my world have talked about the "silver tsunami," the idea that baby-boomer owners would retire and flood the market with businesses for sale. The demographics are real. More than half of U.S. small business owners are now over 55. McKinsey expects about six million businesses to change ownership by 2035, with more than a million of them viable for sale and up to $5 trillion in enterprise value at stake.

The flood never came, though. BizBuySell's data shows closed small business sales in Q2 2026 were down about 10% from a year earlier. A Forbes piece this September put it well: the silver tsunami is becoming a *sorting event*. Some businesses will sell, some will transfer to family or employees, and many will close.

What decides which group you land in? It's usually not your industry or your revenue. It's whether a stranger can run the business without you.

## Why buyers walk away

Over the last few years, I've walked through a lot of small businesses with owners, looking at them the way a buyer or a lender would. The deals that die almost always die for one of the same five reasons.

1. **The earnings don't hold up.** Add-backs that make sense to the owner fall apart under a lender's review. Personal expenses run through the business, and so does revenue that won't recur.
2. **The owner is the business.** The owner holds the customer relationships, prices the jobs and fixes the equipment. Without the owner, the buyer is paying for a job, not a company.
3. **Nothing is written down.** Ask how an order gets from phone call to invoice, and the answer is "Linda handles that." Linda is 63.
4. **One customer is too big a share of revenue.** One general contractor or one distributor relationship might be 40% of the business, held together by a handshake.
5. **Financing breaks the deal.** This got harder on October 1. Under the SBA's updated rules, acquisition loans now need an independent valuation, debt coverage of 1.25x *based on historical earnings rather than projections*, and a quality-of-earnings report on larger deals. Nearly 8 in 10 small business buyers plan to use SBA financing, so a business that can't pass lender diligence has very few buyers left.

Notice that none of those five is "the business wasn't good." Most of these companies are good, with loyal customers, skilled crews and real margins. They just aren't *transferable*.

## Transferable is a design choice

It took me a long time to understand this, but transferability isn't something you discover when you list the business. You build it, years earlier, through hundreds of small operating decisions.

- Every process you document is one less thing a buyer has to take on faith.
- Every customer relationship you move from you to a team member is one less thing that walks out the door with you.
- Every manual workflow you turn into a reliable system is one less key person a buyer has to underwrite.
- Every clean month of books makes it easier for a lender to say yes.

The market pays for this. BizBuySell's Q3 2026 data shows businesses selling for under $1 million averaged about **2.3 times** cash flow, while businesses above $1 million averaged about **3.7 times**. Part of that gap is size, but a lot of it is quality: cleaner earnings, less dependence on the owner and more systems. Buyers are paying up for earnings they can transfer and discounting everything else.

## The 24-month window

If you're an owner thinking about retiring within the next five years, and Chase's 2026 research says 40% of owners expect to retire within a decade, here's the honest math. The work that makes a business sellable takes 12 to 24 months to show up in your financials and your operations, and buyers want to see a track record, not a promise.

So the best time to start was three years ago. The second best time is this quarter.

Here's where I'd begin.

1. **Write down your week.** List every task you personally touched in the last five working days, and mark which ones *only* you can do. That list is your owner-dependence map.
2. **Pick the three processes that matter most:** usually quoting, order-to-invoice and collections. Document them well enough that a new hire could follow them.
3. **Clean up your books for a buyer, not just for taxes.** Separate personal spending, and understand which add-backs a lender would actually accept.
4. **Move one key customer relationship** to someone else on your team this year.
5. **Automate the double entry.** Wherever data gets typed twice, fix it. Clean data and a lighter administrative load make the business easier to run, easier to diligence and more valuable.
