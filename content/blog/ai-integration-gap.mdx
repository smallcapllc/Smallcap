This year I read every small business AI survey I could find, and they all say the same thing in different words.

Goldman Sachs surveyed its 10,000 Small Businesses community in early 2026, and 76% of owners said they use AI. Only 14% said it's fully built into their core operations. Gusto's fall survey found that two-thirds of owners with fewer than 25 employees use AI in some way, but only 5% say it's built into every part of the business where it could be. Intuit's 2026 report, drawn from payment data at more than five million businesses, found that roughly 7 in 10 use AI regularly while only about 1 in 10 pay for a dedicated AI tool.

So almost everyone has tried it, and almost nobody has rebuilt a workflow around it.

I call that the integration gap, and it's the reason Small Cap exists. I don't think closing it is a technology problem. I think it's an operations problem, and a very old one.

## What "using AI" usually means

When I sit with the owner of a legacy B2B company, like a contractor, distributor or machine shop that has been around for 30 years, and ask how they use AI, I usually hear one of three answers.

- "My office manager uses ChatGPT to clean up emails."
- "My son set something up for our social media."
- "We tried a tool for estimates, but nobody kept using it."

None of those is wrong, but none of them changes the economics of the business. The money in a company like that leaks somewhere else: from the purchase order that gets typed in twice, the invoice that sits in someone's inbox for nine days, the change order nobody documented, and the estimate that only the owner knows how to price.

That's where AI pays off, and it's exactly where the surveys show almost nobody has put it to work.

## Why the gap exists

I don't think owners are lazy or behind. I think three things get in their way.

**1. The tools are sold one task at a time, but the work flows across steps.** A chatbot can write an email, but it doesn't know that this email confirms a PO, which has to land in QuickBooks, which kicks off a materials order, which should alert the project manager. The value sits in the handoffs, and most tools only touch one step.

**2. Nobody has written the process down.** You can't automate what you can't describe. In most legacy companies the real process lives in two or three people's heads, and the written version, if one exists, is from 2014.

**3. Nobody owns it.** In a company with 20 employees, there's no Director of Operations Technology. The owner is quoting jobs. The office manager is buried, so "we should look into AI" stays on the whiteboard for a year.

The surveys say the same thing. In Goldman's data, 73% of owners said they'd benefit from more training and implementation help. The barriers they named were a lack of technical expertise, difficulty choosing tools and data privacy. None of those is really about the technology. They're about capacity.

## What closing the gap looks like

When I say AI belongs in operations, I mean something concrete. Here's a pattern we design for over and over in back offices like these.

1. **A purchase order arrives by email.** It might be a PDF, a scan, or text typed into the body of the message.
2. **An agent pulls out the fields:** customer, PO number, line items, quantities, ship-to address and requested date.
3. **It checks them against the system of record**, whether that's QuickBooks, an ERP, or the spreadsheet that runs the shop. Does the customer exist? Do the item codes match? Does the price match the quote?
4. **A person approves the exceptions.** Clean orders flow through, and anything odd is flagged to a named person with the reason.
5. **The order gets entered, and the confirmation goes back** to the customer, logged against the job.

Nothing about that is futuristic. Construction software companies now publish case studies of general contractors processing a month of bills in a day instead of a week and a half, with a person still approving every transaction before it posts. BDO's 2026 forecast for real estate and construction says the biggest effect of AI this year will be on back-office work like contracts, compliance and scheduling.

Look at the design in that pattern, though: **the person stays in charge, and the AI does the typing.** That's the version that works in a business where one wrong invoice can sour a 20-year customer relationship.

## The three questions I ask first

Before I recommend any tool, I want answers to three questions.

- **Where does the same information get typed twice?** That's usually the fastest payback.
- **Where does work wait?** Look for the inbox, desk or person where things stack up. Cycle time is cash.
- **What only the owner can do, and why?** This is the hardest question, and it's the one that matters most for what the business is worth someday.

If you can answer those three, you already know more about where AI belongs in your company than most software vendors will ever learn.
