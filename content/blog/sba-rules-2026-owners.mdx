If you own a small business and think you'll sell it within the next five years, the most important news of 2026 probably wasn't about AI or interest rates. It was a dry SBA procedures update that took effect on October 1.

Most owners haven't heard of it. Their future buyers' lenders have.

*I'm not a lender or a lawyer, and you should confirm specifics with your banker, CPA and deal attorney. This is how I read the change as someone who helps owners get their operations ready for a buyer and a lender.*

## Why the SBA matters to your exit

At the size most of my readers operate, roughly $1 million to $10 million in revenue, the buyer for your business is probably an individual operator, a searcher or a small holding company, and that buyer is probably financing the deal with an SBA 7(a) loan. BizBuySell's 2026 surveys found that nearly 8 in 10 small business buyers expect to use SBA financing. Brokers named SBA financing as the top obstacle to closing deals, ahead of interest rates.

So the SBA's rules decide who can buy your business and how much they can pay. When the rules tighten, the pool of buyers who can actually close gets smaller.

## What changed on October 1

Based on the published summaries of the updated SOP, here are the parts that matter for a change-of-ownership loan.

1. **Debt coverage went up, and it's based on history.** The required debt service coverage ratio went from 1.15x to 1.25x, *calculated on the business's historical earnings, not projections.* A buyer can no longer justify the loan with the growth plan they intend to carry out after closing.
2. **Every acquisition loan needs an independent valuation.**
3. **A quality-of-earnings report is required** on initial acquisitions and expansions of $3 million or more.
4. **The simpler path is gone.** All acquisitions now go through standard 7(a) underwriting.

## What that does to your price

The new debt coverage rule affects your sale price directly.

Picture a business with $400,000 in seller's discretionary earnings. A buyer has to pay themselves a reasonable salary, so the lender might count roughly $300,000 as available to cover debt. At 1.15x coverage, that supports about $261,000 a year in debt payments. At 1.25x, it supports about $240,000. That $21,000 drop in annual debt capacity translates into a meaningfully smaller loan, often well over $100,000 less depending on the rate and term. That gap has to be filled with more buyer equity, more seller financing or a lower price.

*These figures are illustrative, not loan quotes.* The direction is what matters: **every dollar of earnings a lender won't accept is now worth more to you than it was in September.**

## The add-back problem

This is where most owners get hurt. Add-backs are the expenses you run through the business that a new owner wouldn't have, like your truck, your family's phone plan or the one-time lawsuit. Owners count them generously. Lenders and independent valuators often don't.

Under stricter, history-based underwriting, the earnings that count are the ones that are:

- **Documented**, with receipts, invoices and reconciled bank statements
- **Consistent**, showing up across two or three years rather than in one good year
- **Separable**, clearly business or clearly personal, with no gray area
- **Repeatable**, coming from customers and work that will still be there after you leave

If your books need a 40-minute explanation from your bookkeeper, a valuator will discount them, and the buyer's lender will lend against the discounted number.

## The back office is now part of your valuation

This is the part almost nobody says out loud. **The quality of your back office now directly affects your exit price.**

A quality-of-earnings review is basically someone trying to tie your revenue to real invoices, your invoices to real payments and your costs to real jobs. In many legacy businesses, that trail runs through paper tickets, email attachments, a spreadsheet only one person understands and a QuickBooks file with five years of "Ask My Accountant" entries.

Every gap in that trail costs you something: weeks of diligence delay, a lower valuation, a bigger escrow holdback or a buyer who walks away.

That's why I've come to think of back-office automation as exit preparation. When purchase orders, invoices, bills and job costs move through a consistent, documented, timestamped system:

- Revenue ties to source documents automatically.
- Job-level margins are visible without a forensic project.
- Customer concentration and retention are easy to show.
- Your earnings history looks like what it is: real.

## A 12-month plan for a lender-ready business

If you're 2 to 5 years from selling, here's what I'd do starting now.

1. **Months 1–2:** Get a CPA who works on sell-side deals to do a mock quality-of-earnings review on your last 24 months. Find out which add-backs survive.
2. **Months 1–3:** Separate personal and business spending completely, going forward and for good.
3. **Months 2–4:** Fix the source-document trail for revenue so every invoice ties to a PO, a job and a payment. This is where automation pays for itself fastest.
4. **Months 3–6:** Close the books monthly, on time, every month. Lenders love boring consistency.
5. **Months 6–12:** Build two to three years of clean, history-based earnings. The rule now favors owners who start early.
