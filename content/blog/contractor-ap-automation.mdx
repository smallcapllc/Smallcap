When contractors ask me about AI, they usually mean the job site: drones, robots, computer vision on a camera feed. Those are real, but they're expensive and a long way off for a 25-person company.

The money is in the office, in the stack of supplier bills, the receipts texted from the field, the certificates of insurance that expire without anyone noticing, and the controller who spends a week and a half every month typing all of it into QuickBooks or Foundation.

This isn't just my opinion. Wipfli's 2026 analysis of AI in construction says the greatest near-term opportunity is in the back office. BDO's 2026 construction forecast expects the biggest effect on self-contained back-office tasks like contracts, compliance and scheduling. A UK survey by the Federation of Master Builders and CIOB found that 80% of construction firms using AI are using it for administration.

The case studies are getting specific. One software vendor published the results from a $40 million general contractor: a month of bills used to take about a week and a half, and now it takes one day, with the CFO still reviewing every transaction. Another describes a $20 million contractor that lost a receipt filer and an office assistant and covered both roles with automation instead of rehiring.

So why do most contractors still do accounts payable by hand? Because they get three things wrong.

## Mistake 1: Buying a tool before mapping the flow

AP in a contracting company isn't one task. It's a chain of steps:

1. A bill arrives by email, by mail or inside a supplier portal.
2. Someone figures out which job it belongs to.
3. Someone codes it to the right cost code.
4. A project manager confirms the materials actually showed up.
5. The controller checks it against the PO or the subcontract.
6. It gets entered, approved and scheduled for payment.
7. Lien waivers and COIs get checked before the check goes out.

Most "AI for AP" tools handle steps 1 through 3. If steps 4 through 7 still run on sticky notes and hallway conversations, you've automated the easy part and kept the bottleneck.

**What to do instead:** draw the chain on a whiteboard with your controller and one PM before you look at any software. Mark where bills *wait*. In my experience, the delay is almost always step 4, because the PM is on a job site and the approval request is buried in their email.

## Mistake 2: Letting the AI post to the books

This is the one that scares controllers, and they're right to be scared.

The right design, and the one the credible vendors use, is that **agents prepare the work and people approve it.** The agent captures the bill, reads the vendor, amount, job and line items, suggests a cost code and routes it. The PM approves with one click from their phone. The controller reviews before anything reaches the general ledger.

Nothing posts on its own. Nothing gets paid on its own.

That isn't a limit on the technology. It's how you keep a controller's trust and an auditor's. It also protects your books, which, as I wrote earlier this week, are now part of what your business is worth under the SBA's stricter lending rules.

## Mistake 3: Forgetting the field

The other half of AP is the field. Receipts from the supply house get texted to a group chat, photographed on the dashboard or found crumpled in a truck in March.

The fix isn't another app crews will ignore. It's meeting them where they already are:

- They text a photo to a number, or post it in the chat they already use.
- An agent reads the receipt, matches it to the person, card and likely job, and asks one question if something is unclear, like "Which job is this for?"
- A missing receipt triggers an automatic reminder after 48 hours, so the controller doesn't have to chase it.

COIs work the same way. An agent files each certificate as it arrives, tracks expiration dates and warns you 30 days ahead, so you don't discover a lapsed sub's coverage during an audit or, worse, after a claim.

## What "AP in a day" looks like

For a contractor with $10 million to $40 million in revenue on QuickBooks Online, Foundation or a similar system, here's the target state I design toward:

| Step | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Bill capture | Controller opens every email and envelope | Agent captures everything from a dedicated inbox and supplier portals |
| Job and cost coding | Controller guesses, then asks the PM | Agent suggests codes from past history, and the controller confirms |
| PM approval | Email chain, days of delay | One-tap mobile approval with an automatic reminder |
| Entry | Typed by hand | Exported or imported after approval |
| Receipts | Group chat and the glovebox | Text a photo, matched automatically, chased automatically |
| COIs | Filing cabinet | Filed, tracked and flagged before expiration |

The metrics I'd track are **days to process a month of bills, hours of controller time per week, bills older than 10 days that still need approval, and receipts missing at month-end.** If those four numbers move, the project worked.
