People ask me how a firm as small as Small Cap can research leads, write proposals, produce client deliverables, track cash and publish content every week. The honest answer is that I don't do most of it by hand anymore. I run the company with a team of specialized AI agents, and I treat them the way I'd treat a team of people: clear roles, clear inputs, one source of truth and a human signing off on anything that matters.

I'm sharing the actual structure because I think it's where small business is going, and because the lessons apply whether you run a consultancy, a contracting company or a distributor.

## The org chart

Here's how the work is divided today.

| Role | What it does | Cadence | What it never does |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Chief of Staff** | Coordinates the other agents, keeps the task list honest, handles one-off projects | On demand | Commit money or send anything without my approval |
| **Scout** (market research) | Finds and enriches legacy B2B leads, like North Carolina contractors, and drafts first-touch outreach | Every weekday morning, capped at 10 new leads a day | Send an email. Drafts wait for me |
| **Architect** (proposals) | Turns a discovery call transcript into a proposal the same day, logged against the deal | When a call happens | Invent scope or pricing outside our three offers |
| **Delivery** (the analyst) | Produces client audits, SOPs and 30-60-90 day plans to a fixed standard | When a deal closes | Guess. Missing data gets flagged for the client to verify |
| **CFO** (the auditor) | Weekly scoreboard covering revenue, pipeline, owner earnings and tax milestones | Sunday evenings | Make up numbers when an account isn't connected |
| **Content Studio** | Schedules and publishes content across channels | On a schedule | Post anything off-brand without review |

Six roles, and I'm the only human.

## The rules that make it work

The agents themselves aren't the interesting part. The rules around them are. These are the ones I learned the hard way.

**1. One source of truth.** Early on, two of our agents built two different CRMs, so we had duplicate deals and two sets of prices. Now everything lives in one four-tab CRM, with companies, contacts, deals and activities, plus a dashboard tab on top. Every agent reads from it and writes to it. If something isn't in the CRM, it didn't happen. This is the most important rule, and it's the same rule I'd give any 20-person contractor: pick one system of record and make everything else feed it.

**2. Fixed prices, written down once.** Our Implementation Sprint is $2,500. When that price changed, I said it once, in one place, and every agent picked it up. Ambiguity costs time and money. Agents amplify whatever you give them, including your inconsistency.

**3. Humans approve anything external.** No agent sends an email, posts publicly or spends money without my explicit approval. Scout drafts outreach, and I send it. This costs me maybe 20 minutes a day, and it's the cheapest insurance I've ever bought. The 2026 survey data says the same thing: Pax8 found the small businesses getting the most out of AI are the most demanding ones, with approval steps, human review and named accountability.

**4. Every deliverable has a definition of done.** Our delivery agent works from a written standard: a three-bullet executive summary, tables instead of paragraphs, specific KPIs, named tools, numbered SOP steps with an owner for each, and a 30-60-90 day schedule. Anything it can't verify goes into a highlighted "client verification needed" box so a human can check the whole document in under 15 minutes. That standard matters more than the model.

**5. Low usage by default.** Agents run when there's work, not constantly. Our proposal agent sits idle until a call happens. Our CFO runs once a week. Running everything on a set schedule keeps costs predictable and makes the output easy to review.

## What it's actually like

I won't pretend it's magic. Here's what changed for me.

- **My job is now mostly judgment.** I decide which leads are worth a call, which proposals go out and what we will and won't do. The typing, formatting, researching and reconciling are mostly done before I see them.
- **Mistakes are visible.** Every action gets logged to a deal ID in the activities tab, so when something goes wrong I can see exactly where.
- **Bad instructions show up fast.** If an agent produces something wrong, the cause is usually that I was vague. Writing instructions for agents has made me a clearer manager of people too.

What hasn't changed is that the relationship with a client is still mine. Nobody hires a consultant because of the CRM.

## Why this matters for a legacy business

You don't need six agents. Most of the owners I talk to need one or two to start, set up in the same disciplined way:

- **One system of record** that the automation reads from and writes to.
- **One narrow job**, like capturing invoices, entering POs or tracking certificates of insurance, with a clear definition of done.
- **A human approval step** before anything reaches a customer, a vendor or the general ledger.
- **Someone accountable for keeping it running**, because tools change, vendors change and your process will change.

That last point is where most small business automation dies. Someone builds a workflow, the person who understood it leaves, a field gets renamed in QuickBooks, and three months later everyone is back to typing.
