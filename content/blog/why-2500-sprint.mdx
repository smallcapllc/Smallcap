I've changed the price of our Implementation Sprint more than once, and I settled on **$2,500**.

People in consulting tell me that's too low. Owners of legacy businesses sometimes tell me it sounds too good to be true. Both reactions are why I want to explain the reasoning. It's a founder decision I've thought hard about, and the logic applies to anyone selling services to small businesses.

## The problem with how consulting is usually priced

Most consulting for small businesses is priced in one of two ways.

- **Hourly or time-and-materials.** The owner can't predict the bill, the consultant gets paid more when things take longer, and both sides know it.
- **Big custom projects.** A $25,000 to $60,000 "digital transformation" proposal with a 40-page scope, sold to a business that has never spent more than $5,000 on anything technical.

For the owner of a $6 million contracting company, both are hard to say yes to. Hourly feels risky. A big project feels like a gamble, because they've usually been burned by software that nobody ended up using.

The survey data backs this up. Owners say the main barriers to AI are lack of expertise, difficulty choosing tools and unclear return. Intuit's 2026 data shows most small businesses are still in a free, experimental phase, and only about 1 in 10 pay for a dedicated AI tool. These owners aren't ready to write a five-figure check to a stranger. They *are* ready to fix one painful thing.

## What $2,500 buys

The sprint is deliberately narrow:

- **One workflow.** PO intake, invoicing, AP approvals, estimates, COI tracking or whatever hurts most.
- **One map.** We document how the work actually flows today, including who touches it, where it waits and where it gets typed twice.
- **One build.** A working automation connected to the systems you already use, with a person approving anything that reaches a customer, vendor or your books.
- **One handoff.** A written process, a named owner on your team and a short metrics baseline, so you can see whether it worked.

It doesn't include rebuilding your whole company, replacing your ERP or delivering a 40-page strategy deck.

## Why a small fixed price works

**1. It turns a gamble into an experiment.** At $2,500, an owner doesn't need to believe in AI. They only need to believe their AP backlog or PO double entry costs more than $2,500 a quarter, and it almost always does. A controller spending 15 hours a week on manual entry costs far more than that in a single month.

**2. It forces discipline on us.** A fixed price for a fixed scope means we can't hide inefficiency in billable hours. We had to build a repeatable delivery system: standard intake, a standard mapping format, a standard deliverable with KPIs, numbered SOP steps, owners, a 30-60-90 day schedule and flagged items for the client to verify. A lot of that system runs on the AI agents I wrote about a few weeks ago. A low price only works if delivery is efficient.

**3. It earns the right to a second conversation.** We'd rather prove ourselves on one workflow than sell a promise. If the sprint works, the next conversation is about keeping it running or tackling the next bottleneck. If it doesn't, the client is out $2,500, not $40,000, and we've learned something.

**4. It fits how small businesses buy.** Many owners can approve $2,500 on a card the same week. Anything much bigger usually means a spouse conversation, a CPA conversation and a three-month delay.

## How it fits the business model

I'm open about the ambition: I want Small Cap to grow into a firm with an eight-figure-scale goal, one that pays its owner well with a small team and a lot of leverage. A one-time sprint, by itself, would never get there. No matter how many we sold, we'd start every month back at zero.

The sprint is the front door, not the whole house. It's built to lead into two ongoing relationships:

| Offer | Type | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Implementation Sprint** | One-time | Prove value on one workflow |
| **Managed Care** | Ongoing | Keep the automations alive, monitored and updated |
| **Advisory Retainer** | Ongoing | Act as an operations partner for owners and acquirers |

The recurring work is what turns a busy consultancy into a durable business. Each sprint is a low-risk way for a client to find out whether they want an ongoing relationship, and for us to find out whether we're the right fit. If a sprint doesn't lead anywhere, it still has to be worth its price on its own.

## What I had to accept

Pricing this way meant giving some things up.

- **Saying no to scope creep, kindly and every time.** "That's a great second sprint" is a phrase I use a lot.
- **Not taking every client.** If a business has no system of record at all, the first step isn't automation, and I'll tell them that.
- **Running a tighter operation than a typical boutique.** One CRM, one price list, one delivery standard and a weekly scoreboard. A low price leaves no room for mess.

## The lesson for other founders

If you sell to small businesses, price for the decision your customer can actually make this week. A small, fixed, narrow offer that delivers a visible result beats a big, custom, open-ended one almost every time, as long as you build the delivery system that makes it profitable.
