Most contractors I talk to have something in common. Somewhere on a phone, in a shared drive or in a truck's camera roll, they have years of photos of finished work. There are before-and-afters of a warehouse roof. There's a clean panel install. There's a shop floor they reorganized for a manufacturer in 2019. Each one got shown to someone once, maybe in a single social post that week, and then disappeared.

That archive is some of the most persuasive marketing a legacy business owns, and almost nobody uses it twice.

I started thinking about this after reading [a post by photography coach Alexandra Peterson](https://alexandrapeterson.com/how-to-get-eyes-on-old-instagram-posts/) on getting more out of old social posts. Her point was simple: a post's useful life is short, but the work behind it isn't. If you group older pieces by theme and give people a reason to browse them, they start working for you again. She was writing for photographers. I think the idea matters even more for a 30-year-old company with a 30-year track record and almost nothing on its website to show for it.

## Why this matters for a legacy business

A buyer choosing between three bids wants one thing above all: proof you've done *this kind of job* before. They want to see the same scope, the same building type and the same headaches handled well.

You almost certainly have that proof. It's just scattered:

- **Project photos** in phones, CompanyCam, Google Photos and old email attachments
- **Testimonials** buried in thank-you emails and text messages
- **Case studies**, even informal ones, in old proposals and closeout packets
- **Answers to the same 15 questions**, written over and over in customer emails
- **Old posts and newsletters** that went out once and were never seen again

The problem isn't a shortage of material. The problem is that nothing resurfaces it. Every new post starts from a blank page, while the best evidence you have sits in a folder.

## The fix: a library, themed collections and a schedule

The system has three parts. None of them is complicated, and the first two require no AI at all.

### 1. Build one content library

Pick one place to be the home for reusable proof. For most small companies, a Google Sheet or an Airtable base is plenty. Each row is one asset, with these columns:

- Asset type (photo set, testimonial, case study, FAQ answer, old post)
- Project or customer, plus the date
- Service line (for example, commercial roofing, tenant buildout or preventive maintenance)
- Building or industry type
- Location (city or county)
- Link to the file
- Permission status (do we have the customer's OK to share this?)
- Last used, and where

The last two columns matter most. Permission keeps you out of trouble. "Last used" is what makes resurfacing possible.

### 2. Group assets into themed collections

This is the core of Peterson's idea, translated to our world. One random photo doesn't say much. Eight photos of the same kind of job, put together in one place, tell a buyer you're a specialist.

Collections that tend to work well for legacy B2B companies:

1. **By service:** "Commercial re-roofs we've completed since 2018"
2. **By customer type:** "Work for distribution centers and warehouses"
3. **By location:** "Projects in Wake and Durham counties"
4. **By problem solved:** "Fixing water intrusion in metal buildings"
5. **By question:** "What a tenant buildout timeline really looks like"

Each collection can live in several places: a page on your website, a featured section on LinkedIn, a Google Business Profile update, a one-page PDF attached to a proposal, or a short email to past customers. You make it once and use it many times.

### 3. Put resurfacing on a schedule, and let AI do the busywork

This is where a little automation turns a good intention into a habit. Here's an illustrative weekly flow for a 20-person contractor:

1. **Monday morning:** an automation in Zapier or Make picks two library rows that have permission and haven't been used in 90+ days, rotating through your service lines.
2. **An AI assistant drafts the copy.** Using the job details in the row, ChatGPT or Claude writes a short LinkedIn post, a Google Business Profile update and a two-line caption, all in your company's voice from a saved style guide.
3. **A person approves it.** The office manager or owner gets a single message: approve, edit or skip. Nothing goes public without that step.
4. **Approved posts get scheduled** through a tool such as Buffer or Postiz, spread across the week.
5. **The library updates itself.** "Last used" gets stamped, so the same asset doesn't come back for another quarter.

Once a month, the same flow can assemble a themed collection, like "five recent warehouse projects," into a simple email for past customers and a PDF for your estimators to attach to bids.

The total weekly human time I'd target is **15 minutes of review.** The rest is lookup, drafting and scheduling, which is exactly the kind of repetitive work AI and simple automations handle well.

## Two numbers to watch

Keep the measurement simple. I'd track two things:

- **Assets resurfaced per month.** A practical early target is 8 to 12. If this number drops to zero, the system has quietly died.
- **Proposals sent with matching proof attached.** What share of your bids include photos or a short case study of a similar job? Getting this from "rarely" to "most" is often the quickest way to make your bids feel lower-risk to the buyer.

If you want a third number later, ask new leads how they found you, and count the ones who mention a project page or a post.

## A few guardrails

- **Get permission.** Customer names, logos and building photos often need an OK, especially for commercial and government work. Track it in the library.
- **Keep it honest.** AI can draft captions, but the facts, like scope, dates and results, come from your records. If a detail isn't in the row, it doesn't go in the post.
- **Watch out for security-sensitive details.** Skip photos that show alarm panels, access points or anything a customer wouldn't want public.
