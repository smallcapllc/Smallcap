The first conversation with a potential seller often matters more than the first model. In succession-driven acquisitions, owners are not only evaluating price. They are evaluating whether you will protect what they spent a lifetime building—and whether they will regret taking your call.

Trust is the real gate. The term sheet comes later.

## Begin as a student of the craft, not a hunter of the asset

Cold outreach that reads like a mass mailer ("We acquire businesses with $X of EBITDA") signals that the owner is inventory. A better opening shows you have done specific homework:

- You understand the niche
- You know what customers care about
- You respect what is distinctive about their company
- You are exploring fit, not demanding a process

Curiosity without flattery. Specificity without surveillance. That tone is rare enough to earn a reply.

## Lead with stewardship questions

Early meetings go wrong when buyers rush to valuation. Early meetings go right when buyers learn the owner's definition of a good outcome.

Useful questions:

- What are you most proud of in this business?
- What would you want preserved no matter who owns it?
- Which people would you want to see thrive after a transition?
- What would a successful next chapter look like for you personally?
- What concerns you most about selling—beyond price?

Listen longer than feels efficient. Owners can tell when you are waiting to speak.

## Make your process transparent

Uncertainty breeds suspicion. Explain, in plain language:

- How you evaluate opportunities
- What a typical timeline looks like
- Who will be involved on your side
- What confidentiality means in practice
- How you handle a "no"—including theirs

Clarity is a form of respect. It also filters for sellers who want a professional process rather than a vague courtship with no end.

## Do not manufacture urgency

Artificial deadlines, exploding offers, and "we have another deal this week" pressure tactics can win a signature and lose the transition. In owner-operated businesses, post-close cooperation is often part of the value. An owner who feels cornered can comply on paper and disengage in spirit.

If there is a real reason for pace—estate timing, health, a competing process—name it honestly. If there is not, do not invent one.

## Bring the right people into the room at the right time

Many owners want their spouse, partner, accountant, or attorney involved earlier than financial buyers expect. That is not friction. That is how family businesses make decisions.

Invite it. A deal that surprises the household often collapses later—or closes with resentment that shows up in transition.

When advisors join, treat them as partners in a careful decision, not obstacles to overcome.

## Show how you handle people risk

Sellers worry about their teams. Address it with substance:

- How you think about retention
- What changes you typically make in the first year—and what you do not
- How you communicate with employees after signing
- Examples of transitions you are willing to describe at a high level (without breaching confidence)

You do not need to promise that nothing will change. You need to show that change will be deliberate.

## Align incentives with behavior you want after closing

Earnouts, consulting agreements, and rollovers can help—or can create conflict.

Design them so that:

- The seller is rewarded for a real handoff, not for clinging to control
- Goals are measurable and not easy to game
- Both sides can explain the deal simply to employees
- Disputes have a clear resolution path

Complicated structures that nobody can explain usually encode mistrust. Prefer clean economics and clear roles.

## Put culture into diligence, not into a footnote

Visit the floor. Meet the managers. Observe how the owner treats people when nothing is being sold. Ask what rituals, standards, and informal rules make the place work.

Then be honest about what you would keep. Owners can accept change. They struggle with buyers who praise the culture in January and discard it in March.

## Know when to walk away early

Walking away cleanly is part of earning a reputation. If fit is wrong—on values, on risk, on transition willingness—say so before both sides sink months into diligence theater.

A graceful pass often leads to a later introduction. A messy almost-deal becomes a warning story told to every other owner in the network.

## Close as partners in a transfer of stewardship

When you do reach terms, frame the moment accurately. You are not conquering a target. You are accepting responsibility for a living organization: customers who rely on it, employees who built it, and an owner who is choosing to trust you with both.

That posture is not sentimentality. In small-cap markets, it is strategy. Network of trust is not a tagline when succession is involved. It is the operating system that makes proprietary deals possible—and makes closed deals succeed.
