There's one question I ask every owner, and it tells me more about what their business is worth than the P&L does:

**If you disappeared for 30 days, with no phone, no email and no "quick question," what percentage of the business would keep running normally?**

Most owners answer quickly and confidently. Then, when we go through it task by task, the real number is usually much lower.

Business brokers have a version of this they call the 80% test: buyers want to see that at least 80% of the operation runs without the owner. Whether the threshold is 70% or 85%, the principle holds up in every buyer conversation. **The more the business depends on you, the less of it a buyer can actually buy.**

## Why buyers care so much

When someone buys a business that depends heavily on its owner, they're buying a risk. They're betting that customer relationships, pricing judgment, vendor terms and tribal knowledge will transfer during a transition period, usually 30 to 90 days of the seller "helping out."

Buyers protect themselves from that risk in one of three ways:

- **A lower price.** The multiple drops to reflect the risk.
- **A different structure.** More seller financing or an earnout, so you keep carrying the risk after closing.
- **No deal.** They buy the business down the street that runs without its founder.

In a 2026 market that Forbes describes as a "sorting event," where buyers are more selective and SBA lenders underwrite on historical earnings, owner dependence is one of the main reasons good businesses end up unsold.

It also costs you now, not just at exit. A business that can't run without you means you can't take a real vacation, can't get sick and can't step back. The personal cost and the valuation cost come from the same problem.

## The self-assessment

Grab a legal pad and score each area from 0 to 10, where 10 means it runs completely without you and 0 means it stops when you stop.

| Area | Question | Score (0–10) |
|---|---|---|
| **Sales** | Do new customers come in and get closed without you? | |
| **Pricing and estimating** | Can someone else price a typical job accurately? | |
| **Key customers** | Would your top 5 customers stay if you were gone for a month? | |
| **Operations** | Do jobs get scheduled, staffed and completed without your decisions? | |
| **Purchasing and vendors** | Can someone else order, negotiate and resolve vendor issues? | |
| **Finance** | Do invoicing, collections, bills and payroll happen without you? | |
| **Hiring and people** | Can a manager hire, train and handle problems? | |
| **Problem-solving** | When something breaks, does someone other than you fix it? | |
| **Knowledge** | Are the important processes written down somewhere other than your head? | |
| **Systems** | Does information live in a system, or in email, texts and memory? | |

Add it up. That's your rough percentage.

In most owner-operated companies with $2 million to $10 million in revenue, the scores I'd expect cluster around pricing, key customers and problem-solving in the 2-to-4 range. Those three areas are usually the expensive ones, because they're exactly what a buyer is paying for.

## Why it's always the same three

**Pricing** lives in the owner's head because it's built from decades of judgment: what this kind of job really costs, which customers pay slowly and how much to pad for a bad general contractor. Nobody ever wrote it down because it never needed to be.

**Key customers** are loyal to the owner because the owner built those relationships personally, often over 20 years of phone calls.

**Problem-solving** comes back to the owner because, for years, the owner was the fastest person to fix things, so everyone learned to ask.

None of this is a character flaw. It's what happens naturally when a founder builds a business, and it has to be undone deliberately.

## A 90-day plan to raise your score

**Days 1–30: Make it visible.**
1. For two weeks, write down every decision you make and every question you get asked. Don't change anything yet. Just record it.
2. Group the list. You'll find that 60% to 70% of it falls into a handful of repeating categories.
3. Choose the one category that would hurt most if you disappeared. That's your first target.

**Days 31–60: Get it out of your head.**

4. Record yourself doing the task, like pricing three real jobs, and explain your reasoning out loud.
5. Turn that into a written process: numbered steps, decision rules ("if the GC is X, add Y%") and a named owner.
6. Where the task involves moving information between documents, emails and systems, automate that part so your stand-in only has to make the judgment calls, not the data entry.

**Days 61–90: Step back.**

7. Hand the task to the named owner, and review their work instead of doing it.
8. Introduce that person to one key customer as the day-to-day contact.
9. Take a real long weekend: no phone, with someone else on call. Watch what breaks, and fix the process, not the person.

Then score yourself again. Moving from 45% to 60% in a quarter is realistic. Repeat that for two or three quarters, and you'll have a fundamentally different business, both to own and to sell.
