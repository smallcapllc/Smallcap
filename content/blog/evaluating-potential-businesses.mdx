Finding a business for sale is only the first filter. The harder work is deciding whether it deserves capital—and under what structure. In small-cap private investing, the gap between a "good company" and a "good deal" is where returns are made or destroyed.

Below is a practical framework we use to evaluate opportunities. It is not a checklist that replaces judgment. It is a set of questions that force judgment into the open.

## Start with earnings quality, not headline EBITDA

Reported earnings can be tidy and still misleading. Focus on:

- Recurring versus one-time revenue
- Owner add-backs that would not survive professional ownership
- Working capital habits that temporarily inflate cash
- Customer incentives that pull demand forward
- Maintenance capex that has been deferred to polish a sale year

Ask what the business earns in a normal year under a normal owner—not what it earned in the best year under a seller preparing for exit.

## Map concentration risk honestly

Concentration is common in niche businesses. It is not automatically disqualifying. It is a pricing and structure issue.

Examine:

- Top customer share and contract terms
- Supplier dependence for critical inputs
- Geographic concentration
- Key-person concentration in sales or technical delivery
- Channel dependence (one marketplace, one distributor, one referral source)

If one relationship can impair the thesis, the deal must either reduce that risk post-close or compensate for it in price and protections.

## Understand the competitive moat in plain language

Avoid abstract language. Can you explain, in a few sentences, why customers stay?

Useful forms of durability in small markets often include:

- Switching costs embedded in workflows
- Specialized know-how that is hard to hire quickly
- Local density advantages
- Regulatory or certification barriers
- Reputation earned over decades in a trust-sensitive niche

If the only moat is "the owner knows everyone," you are buying a transition project, not a franchise. That can still be a good investment—if underwritten as such.

## Separate the business from the founder

In owner-operated companies, this is often the central diligence question.

Probe:

- Which customer relationships are institutional versus personal?
- Which processes live in the owner's head?
- Who really makes pricing, hiring, and capital decisions today?
- What happens to culture if the founder leaves in month one versus month eighteen?

A business that cannot operate without the seller may still be acquirable with a thoughtful transition plan. A business that only exists because of the seller is a different asset.

## Underwrite the people you are inheriting

Financial models rarely capture people risk well. Spend time with managers. Listen to how frontline employees talk about the company when the owner is not in the room.

Look for:

- A second layer of leadership that can grow
- Compensation structures that align with the outcomes you need
- Open roles that the seller underfilled to keep margins high
- Cultural norms that will clash with professionalization

You are not only buying cash flows. You are inheriting a social system.

## Test the growth story with skepticism and curiosity

Growth narratives are cheap. Evidence is not.

Ask:

- Is growth coming from price, volume, mix, or new products?
- Has the company ever successfully entered an adjacent market?
- What capacity constraints appear first—people, facilities, capital, or demand?
- Which investments were deferred because the owner was preparing to exit?

A preservation-first posture does not reject growth. It rejects growth assumptions that require perfect execution on day one.

## Stress the downside before you fall in love with the upside

Write the bear case explicitly:

- A key customer leaves
- A key manager resigns
- Input costs spike and pricing power is weaker than assumed
- Integration or transition takes twice as long
- The industry cycle turns in year two

If the equity story only works in the base case, the structure is wrong—or the price is.

## Require a clear path to information advantage

In small-cap markets, edge often comes from knowing a niche better than a generalist bidder. After initial screening, ask whether you actually have—or can build—that edge:

- Do you understand how customers buy?
- Do you know the real competitors, including informal ones?
- Can you diligence quality in ways a spreadsheet cannot?

If the answer is no, either partner with someone who has the edge or pass. Paying auction prices without asymmetric insight is how capital gets diluted quietly over time.

## Decide with a written investment memo

A short memo forces clarity:

- Thesis in one paragraph
- Key risks and mitigants
- What must be true in the first year
- Why this team / this structure / this price
- The conditions under which you walk away

If you cannot write it cleanly, you are not ready to commit.

## Preservation first, conviction second

The goal is not to find reasons to say yes. The goal is to find the few situations where the downside is understood, the people risk is manageable, and the opportunity still offers asymmetrical upside.

That is diligence as stewardship—not as theater.
