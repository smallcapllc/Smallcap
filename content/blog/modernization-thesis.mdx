Two numbers from BizBuySell's Q3 2026 report sum up the whole small business acquisition market.

- Businesses that sold for **under $1 million** went for an average of **2.30x** cash flow.
- Businesses that sold for **over $1 million** went for an average of **3.73x**.

The median cash flow in the first group was about $123,000. In the second, it was about $559,000.

The simple explanation is size: bigger businesses are less risky, attract more buyers and qualify for different financing. That's true. I think there's a second explanation that matters more for anyone buying a small business, and it's the core of how we think about acquisitions.

**A big part of the multiple gap is an operations gap. Operations can be fixed.**

## What the higher multiple actually pays for

Ask a buyer why they'll pay 3.7x for one business and 2.3x for another, and you'll hear the same few things:

- **Earnings that hold up to scrutiny.** Clean books, reconciled every month, with add-backs that make sense.
- **Less dependence on the owner.** Managers who run the work, and customers who are loyal to the company rather than one person.
- **Repeatable processes.** Quoting, ordering, invoicing and collections that work the same way every time.
- **Visibility.** Job-level margins, customer-level profitability, a backlog you can see.
- **Room to grow without breaking.** You could add 20% more volume without adding 20% more office staff.

Look at that list. Almost none of it is about the market or the product. It's about how the company is run.

## The thesis

Here's the modernization thesis in one paragraph.

*Buy a durable, owner-operated business with real customers and real margins at a small business multiple. In the first 12 to 24 months, professionalize the operations: document the processes, clean the financials, move relationships from the founder to a team, and automate the back-office work that keeps the company small. Grow earnings modestly, because the operating changes drive most of the value. Then own a business that, on paper and in practice, looks like the kind buyers pay more for.*

Here's the math with deliberately conservative, illustrative numbers.

| | At purchase | After 24 months |
|---|---|---|
| Cash flow (SDE/EBITDA) | $500,000 | $650,000 |
| Multiple | 2.5x | 3.5x |
| Implied value | $1.25M | $2.28M |

Earnings grew 30%, but value grew more than 80%, because the business became a different *kind* of asset. That's the whole idea. *(This is an illustration of the mechanism, not a promise. Multiples depend on industry, size, financing conditions and a dozen other factors.)*

## Why AI changes the math now

This thesis is old. Good operators have been professionalizing small companies for decades. What's new in 2026 is the cost of the operations work.

Five years ago, cleaning up a legacy back office meant hiring a controller, an operations manager and maybe an IT consultant, which could add $250,000 a year in overhead and eat a lot of the earnings growth. Today, much of the repetitive work is handled with AI agents and a person approving the results: PO entry, bill capture and coding, receipt matching, certificate tracking and first drafts of estimates. Contractor case studies this year show a month of bills processed in a day, and two back-office roles covered without rehiring.

That means you can:

1. **Grow without matching headcount**, which shows up directly as margin.
2. **Create the documentation trail** buyers and lenders want, as a side effect of normal operations.
3. **Get the founder out of the details** faster, because the knowledge that lived in their head now lives in the system.

There's also a financing angle. Under the SBA rules that took effect October 1, 2026, acquisition loans are underwritten on historical earnings, with a 1.25x coverage requirement and an independent valuation. A business with a clean, automated earnings trail is easier to buy *and* easier to sell.

## Where acquirers get this wrong

I've watched the modernization playbook go badly, and it usually fails in one of three ways.

**Moving too fast.** A new owner arrives with a software stack and a 30-day plan, the long-time office manager quits in week three, and half the process knowledge leaves with her. I believe in earning trust before changing anything. In your first 100 days, you watch, write things down and fix only what's on fire.

**Automating a broken process.** If quoting is chaotic, automating it gives you faster chaos. Map it, simplify it, and only then automate it.

**Forgetting the people.** The crew, the office and the long-time customers all chose the old owner. Modernization should make their work easier and their jobs more secure. If it feels like surveillance or a threat to their jobs, it will fail, even if the software works.

## The sequence I'd follow

For a newly acquired business with 10 to 50 employees:

1. **Days 1–100:** Learn. Document the top 10 processes as they actually happen. Change nothing except safety and cash emergencies.
2. **Months 4–6:** Fix the books. Monthly close, reconciled, with job costing that people trust.
3. **Months 4–9:** Automate the two workflows with the most double entry, with human approval built in.
4. **Months 6–12:** Move one key customer relationship and one key decision from the founder to a manager.
5. **Months 12–24:** Build a track record. Clean history is what the next buyer pays for.
